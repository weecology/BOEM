Summer 2026 Aerial Survey

Over four days in July, twenty survey flights photographed the ocean surface in continuous overlapping frames. Every one of those half-million images has been searched by an automated detection system. This brief reports the detections we are most confident about.

What the confidence filter leaves out

The frames below are twelve detections from a single image on 12 July, each proposed as a sea duck. At this scale a distant bird and a sunlit wave crest are genuinely hard to tell apart, and similar specks nearby went unmarked. Detections like these are why the confidence filter exists: none of them appears in the 388 counted above, and all of them go to a reviewer instead.

What was seen most

Every detection is put to two independent models — one that names the species directly, one that works down the taxonomic tree. Where they agree, the identification is reported at species level. Where they disagree, it is reported at the rank they still share, or held back entirely.

Marine mammals and turtles

Fewer than in the previous issue of this brief, and that is the intended direction — see the note below.

The whale detections did not survive the model refresh

The previous issue listed eight humpback whales, flagged as unverified. On the refreshed model no whale detection remains anywhere in the survey. The earlier rows came from a classifier trained partly on close-range imagery, which taught it to read size in the frame as a species cue and so to see distant dolphins and wave structure as large whales. That training source has been removed.

The honest reading is that the earlier whale counts were an artefact and are withdrawn, not that whales were present and have now been missed. This model has too few large-whale training images to identify one reliably either way, so absence here is not evidence of absence at sea.

Activity across the survey

12 July accounts for roughly half of the high-confidence detections, on a little under a third of the flying. Detection counts are essentially unchanged from the previous issue; it is the identifications that have moved.

Where these figures come from

Detection figures are measured on two fully-reviewed flights held out of training — 648 frames and 1,229 animals, every one marked by a person. Those flights are from a different, earlier survey than the July flights reported here, and the July imagery is sparser and harder; treat them as a measure of the detector, not as a yield estimate for this survey. Identification figures come from 3,695 held-out image crops spanning all 70 species.

Two known weak points sit inside the common species, and both are being targeted first: Brown Pelican is identified correctly only 24% of the time despite being the seventh commonest animal on record, and Bottlenose Dolphin 30%. Large whales have too few training images to be identified reliably at all, which is why the humpback rows above carry a verification warning.

Species, genus and family accuracy come from the taxonomic model; the per-species breakdown comes from the species classifier. The two agree closely where they overlap — 76.7% and 76.4% at species level — so the two tables can be read together.

Why this is the most important number in the brief

Earlier in the summer the system was set to flag far more generously. The first batch of reviewed frames from that setting came back 34 rejected against 3 confirmed — reviewers were spending almost all their effort on wave crests. After the confidence threshold was re-derived on this survey's own imagery, the same review queue now returns 92 confirmed against 7 rejected. The measured precision from held-out imagery predicted 90%; reviewers are finding 93%, so the two agree closely.

One limit worth stating plainly: reviewers are confirming that an animal is present, not which species it is. Species-level verification has not started, so every species figure in this brief remains machine output. The 3 observations already carried at reviewer-confirmed rank are the exception.

What happens next

This brief will be reissued as each stage completes.

01

Expert verification

Reviewers work through the flagged images, confirming or rejecting each detection and correcting species. 78 of 336 queued frames are done. Marine mammals and turtles go first. This is the step that turns detections into reportable numbers — and that will let us count the many genuine animals currently sitting below the confidence bar.

02

Model refinement

Verified images are fed back into training, which improves both the detector and the species classifier. We expect the largest gains on small birds in rough water, where the system is currently weakest. A second priority is coverage: the model recognises 70 species, and anything outside that list is currently forced into the nearest lookalike rather than flagged as unknown. Sea turtles were the most conspicuous gap and now have a class of their own; the remaining need is a way to say unknown at all.

03

Mapping and density estimates

Flight position records for these twenty flights are still outstanding. Once they are available each detection can be placed geographically, which is what turns counts into distribution maps and density estimates.
